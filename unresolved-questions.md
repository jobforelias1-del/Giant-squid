# Unresolved Questions — Ranked

The major open questions about *Architeuthis dux*, ranked by a combination of scientific importance and how completely the existing evidence fails to answer them. For each: what evidence exists now, and why the question stays open. No novel theories are proposed; entries synthesize the domain findings in `evidence-ledger.csv` (IDs cited).

---

## 1. What does a giant squid do when no one is baiting it?

**Available evidence:** Every deep-water observation ever made (2004 stills; 2012 Medusa and submersible video; 2019 Gulf of Mexico) was elicited by bait, a jig, or a bioluminescence-mimicking lure `[LIV-14]`. The observations establish capacities — active swimming, tentacular strike, stalking of an optical target `[LIV-30]` — not habits.
**Why unresolved:** Unbaited observation requires either luck or long-duration, non-attractive deep platforms; minutes of video (and a few hours of observation) exist against a lifetime of behavior. Natural prey capture, schooling or solitary habits, activity cycles, and predator evasion have never been seen. A 2025 surface-feeding video may be the first natural-feeding record but is unvetted `[LIV-24]`.

## 2. How long does a giant squid live, and how fast does it grow?

**Available evidence:** Statolith increments read as daily give <1–1 year (Lordan et al. 1998; Landman et al. 2004) `[REP-20, REP-21]`; beak increments give ~1.1–1.8 years measured, ~3 years extrapolated (Perales-Raya et al. 2020) `[REP-23]`; growth models give ~3–6 years (Grist & Jackson 2007) `[REP-24]`; bomb-radiocarbon permits up to ~14 years (Landman et al. 2004) `[REP-22]`.
**Why unresolved:** The one-increment-per-day assumption has never been validated for *Architeuthis* — validation requires marking a live animal over known time, and no giant squid has ever been kept alive `[REP-26, REP-31]`. A 14-fold spread in lifespan implies a similar spread in growth rate, mortality, and population turnover; almost every ecological inference downstream depends on this unresolved number.

## 3. Where, when, and how do giant squid spawn — and what happens to the eggs?

**Available evidence:** Ovarian counts of 3.5–6.2 million small oocytes, with multiple size classes suggesting intermittent spawning (Hoving et al. 2004); reported ripe-egg sizes, all under ~2 mm, come from secondary compilations `[REP-11, REP-12]`. Nothing else. No spawning has been observed, no egg mass ever found; the famous diver-filmed gelatinous spheres are *Illex coindetii* `[REP-13, REP-14]`.
**Why unresolved:** Spawning presumably happens at depth, briefly, by animals that die soon after (if the short-lifespan camp is right); fragile gelatinous masses would not survive nets. Paralarvae are known from a handful of specimens (NZ; 6.7–8.8 mm) `[REP-15]`, so a spawning ground must exist near New Zealand — but it has never been located.

## 4. How does fertilization actually work?

**Available evidence:** Females carry implanted spermatangia scattered non-specifically over the body `[REP-06]`; one female's 66 spermatangia all came from one male `[REP-07]`; males self-implant accidentally `[REP-08]`; spermatophores can implant autonomously (shown in another species) `[REP-09]`; moribund males extend the terminal organ through their own funnel `[REP-05]`.
**Why unresolved:** No mating has ever been observed, and no mechanism is known by which sperm embedded in arm skin reach eggs passing through the oviducts `[REP-04, REP-10]`. Whether the modified ventral arm tips function as hectocotyli is also unknown `[REP-02]`.

## 5. What depths does it actually use — and does it migrate vertically?

**Available evidence:** Point observations at 630–900 m plus 759 m in the Gulf of Mexico (camera-platform squid encounters overall spanned 557–950 m, not all *Architeuthis*); NZ trawl bycatch at 400–600 m; isotope-derived lifetime averages of 125–250 m for Tasmanian animals; the textbook "300–1,000 m" is an expert envelope traceable to Roper & Boss (1982) `[DEP-01–DEP-13]`.
**Why unresolved:** No depth-time telemetry has ever been obtained from a living giant squid `[DEP-28]`; the direct-observation depths are where cameras were hung, and capture depths are where fisheries tow. The isotope-vs-observation conflict (shallow averages vs deep encounters) cannot be settled without tracking data, and diel vertical migration remains unverified in either direction.

## 6. How many giant squid are there, and is the population one panmictic pool?

**Available evidence:** Exceptionally low mitochondrial diversity, no geographic structure (43 specimens; Winkelmann et al. 2013), consistent with either recent expansion or a selective sweep — the authors' own ambiguity `[TAX-06–TAX-08]`. A single-individual draft nuclear genome exists but no population-genomic dataset `[TAX-10]`.
**Why unresolved:** mtDNA is one maternally inherited locus; nuclear tests of panmixia have not been published. There is no abundance estimate of any kind — the only quantitative handle (sperm whale consumption arithmetic) carries "discouragingly wide" confidence limits `[SWH-17]`. Conservation status is therefore unassessable.

## 7. What are its sensory capabilities — can it detect an approaching sperm whale?

**Available evidence:** Hearing has been measured only in a small shallow-water squid (30–500 Hz particle motion; deaf to echolocation clicks), and the extension to *Architeuthis* is explicit extrapolation `[SWH-23]`. The giant-eye/whale-detection hypothesis (Nilsson et al. 2012) is contested by allometric analysis (Schmitz et al. 2013), with a rebuttal and no resolution `[DIE-30–DIE-32]`.
**Why unresolved:** Sensory physiology requires live, intact animals — none has ever been available. Field data on deep bioluminescence needed to test the eye models are also sparse.

## 8. What killed the stranded — and what causes stranding clusters?

**Available evidence:** Clusters at Newfoundland (1870s, 1960s), Cook Strait (1879–87), Asturias (2001, 2003), Sea of Japan (2014–15) `[TAX-34]`. Hypotheses on file: warm-water hemocyanin failure (from one 1983 blood experiment) `[DEP-19]`, current-driven "traps" `[DEP-09]`, ~30-year hydrographic cycles `[DEP-25]`, and seismic-survey acoustic trauma (coincidence plus other-species experiments; disputed) `[DEP-21, DEP-22]`.
**Why unresolved:** Every mechanism is inference from occurrence patterns or from n = 1 physiology; none has been tested against independent oceanographic hindcasts or controlled exposures relevant to a deep-sea squid. Necropsies of stranded (decomposing) animals cannot cleanly separate cause from consequence.

## 9. How big do they really get?

**Available evidence:** Verified maximum ~12 m TL (McClain et al. 2015); expert post-mortem-relaxed maximum ~13 m; mantle-length ceiling disputed between 2.25 and 2.79 m; heaviest weighed entire specimen ~220 kg vs expert estimates of 275–300 kg; Paxton (2016) argues 20 m is statistically plausible; specialist rejection of that claim was public, but no formal published rebuttal has been located `[SPE-09–SPE-13, SPE-23, SPE-31]`.
**Why unresolved:** The upper tail of the size distribution rests on the worst-provenance historical data (stretched, paced, second-hand); beak-based estimation carries ~40% spread; and very large females are rare in any sampling stream. Only more measured specimens can close it.

## 10. What is its real place in the sperm whale's economy — and the deep-sea food web?

**Available evidence:** Diet fractions ranging from 0% (North Sea, Mediterranean strandings) through 12.1% by mass (Azores) to 82.2% in one outlier whale `[SWH-02–SWH-08]`; no defensible estimate of giant squid eaten per year; modern faecal eDNA has so far failed to re-detect *Architeuthis* in whale diet `[SWH-29]`.
**Why unresolved:** The whaling-era data stream is closed; beak-based reconstruction carries unquantified biases for very large squid `[SWH-28]`; and predation has never been observed, so whether whales take healthy adults, juveniles, or disproportionately moribund post-spawners is unknown `[SWH-13, SWH-27]`.

## 11. Where exactly does it occur — and are the "gaps" real?

**Available evidence:** A circumglobal temperate map drawn by strandings, bycatch, and predator stomachs; one Mediterranean record; near-absence from tropics and poles; eDNA adding detections (seasonal presence in the Sea of Japan) and new range (Western Australia) `[TAX-19, TAX-23, TAX-27, TAX-33]`.
**Why unresolved:** The map partly maps observers (coastal populations, fisheries, stranding networks). Tropical deep water is barely sampled by any *Architeuthis*-detecting method; absence there is currently unfalsifiable without systematic eDNA or camera surveys.

## 12. Smaller but persistent puzzles

- Why were the 2006 and 2012 Ogasawara animals missing or damaged in one or both feeding tentacles? `[LIV-05, LIV-08]`
- Is cannibalism real behavior or artifact (autophagy, capture stress)? `[DIE-03, DIE-05]`
- What do paralarvae eat, and what eats them? `[open in all domains]`
- Can parasite life-cycles be used systematically to map predators and prey? `[DIE-34]`
- Bibliographic loose ends that block precision: the exact contents of R. Clarke (1955), the primary documentation of USS *Stein* (1976), the Comptes Rendus text of the *Alecton* report, the first source to print "18 m" as a scientific maximum, and page-level verification of Heuvelmans' size claims (see `source-quality-audit.md`).

## The common denominator

Nearly every entry above reduces to the same three structural facts: **(1)** the animal's habitat is dark, deep, and expensive to observe; **(2)** the specimen record is biased toward the dying (buoyant carcasses, coastal observers, fishing gear); and **(3)** no giant squid has ever been held alive, so no assumption — increment periodicity, thermal tolerance, hearing, growth — has ever been validated experimentally. Progress since 2004 has come from engineering around these constraints (unobtrusive optical lures, eDNA, genomics on dead tissue); the open questions are the ones those tools have not yet reached.
